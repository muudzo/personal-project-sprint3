## 1. The project in one paragraph

Sprint 3 is an **open brief**: **I set the problem.** I pick a personal project that is **challenging, fun, and personal**. It has to fit inside one of three **themes** (*Friction!*, *Invisible / Visible*, *Play*) and meet three **hard requirements**: a clear target audience, interactivity in the prototype, and at least one test with people who are not classmates. I choose the methods from the toolbox built up in Sprints 1 and 2.

---

## 2. Hard requirements (non-negotiable)

Every idea, decision, and deliverable gets checked against these:

| # | Requirement | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The design has a clear target audience** | Name a specific group, e.g. "first-year students who doomscroll before bed", not "everyone". |
| 2 | **There is interactivity in the prototype** | The prototype responds to the user (clicks, sensors, physical input, etc.). A static poster or a non-interactive mockup does not count. |
| 3 | **Tested at least once, and not only with classmates** | At least one test session with real people from the target audience outside the class. Document it. |

## 3. Boundaries: the three themes

The project has to sit inside one (or more) of these:

- **Friction!** Deliberately adding resistance, slowness, or effort. Bad UX on purpose, to make people reflect.
- **Invisible / Visible.** Making something hidden perceivable (data, feelings, systems, habits), or hiding something that's normally visible.
- **Play.** Playfulness, games, toys, humour, exploration.

The deck's Venn diagram shows **Challenging + Having fun + Personal**, with **Boundaries** as a circle around all three. The ideal project sits in the centre of the Venn and inside the boundary.

## 4. The three ingredients of a perfect project

> "The perfect project is fun, challenging and personal."

### 4a. Challenging: the Skill × Challenge diagram

The deck plots project types on two axes:

- **X-axis:** Low skill → High skill (how good *I* already am at it)
- **Y-axis:** Small challenge → Big challenge (how hard the project is)

Lecturer's example placements (slide 13):

| Example | Skill | Challenge | Zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coding a website | Low | Big | Anxiety (out of depth) |
| Building a table | Mid–High | Big | Arousal → Flow |
| Riso printing | Mid | Mid | Near the centre |
| Designing a concept for an app | **High** | **Small–Mid** | Control / Relaxation (comfort zone) |

**Key insight:** for interaction design students, "designing an app concept" is *high skill but low challenge*. It's the comfort zone. The brief pushes **away** from that corner.

### 4b. The Flow Model (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi)

The second version of the diagram (slide 15) puts these on Csikszentmihalyi's flow model. Its zones:

| Zone | Skill | Challenge | Feeling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flow** ✅ target | High | High | Fully absorbed, stretched but capable |
| Arousal | Mid | High | Learning fast. The best zone for growth |
| Control | High | Mid | Comfortable, competent |
| Anxiety | Low | High | Overwhelmed |
| Worry | Low | Mid | Uneasy |
| Relaxation | High | Low | Easy, coasting |
| Boredom | Mid | Low | Unengaged |
| Apathy | Low | Low | Nothing at stake |

Lecturer's placements on the flow model: *Setting up my own server* → Anxiety · *Coding a website* → Worry/Apathy edge · *Making illustrations* → centre · *Riso printing* → Arousal/Control edge · *Building a table* → **Flow**.

**Goal for my project:** use skills I'm already **high** in and attach them to a **big** challenge, so the project lands in **Flow** (or in **Arousal** if I'm deliberately learning something new).

## 8. Project status (update as decisions are made)

- **Chosen idea:** **Which Bin?** A web game that teaches the local Dutch waste-sorting rules: drag everyday items into the right bin, with escalating speed and a "real life" mode based on the rules of the tester's own municipality.
- **Theme(s):** Play + Invisible/Visible (the hidden, municipality-specific sorting rules become visible and learnable)
- **Target audience:** International students and expats in the Netherlands who don't know the local sorting rules
- **Skill/Challenge position:** High skill (UI/interaction, usability testing) + big challenge (game design and pacing, a working web build, original research into the rules) → Flow
- **Differentiation:** The existing tools (Afvalwijzer at 1.9/5 from about 1,400 reviews, and Milieu Centraal's Afvalscheidingswijzer) are lookup utilities. Ours is a game that builds a habit. Design decisions taken from their reviews: no login, a "rules last checked" date, no push notifications, and a clear state for a city that isn't covered yet.
- **External test plan (who, where, when):** Remote, by sharing a link, with think-aloud on a video call. Test 1: 2 internationals on the mid-fi Figma screens (Wed 7 Oct). Test 2: 4+ on build v1 (Mon 19 Oct). Test 3: 3+ on build v2 with the database (Thu 22 Oct). Recruit through my own network and international student groups, and book the week-43 testers before the autumn break.

## 9. Rules for any AI helping on this project

1. **Check every suggestion against Section 2 (requirements) and Section 3 (themes).** If an idea fails one, say so.
2. **Push toward Flow.** Ideas should use my existing high skills (interaction/UI design, prototyping, user testing) *and* carry a big challenge. Flag anything that slides into "just an app concept" (comfort zone).
3. **Keep the three ingredients visible:** challenging, fun, personal.
4. **Respect the timeline:** two working weeks (41 and 43), deadline Fri 23 Oct. The autumn break is off.
5. **Testing outside class is mandatory.** Every plan needs a realistic way to reach non-classmates.
6. Update Section 8 when decisions are made.
